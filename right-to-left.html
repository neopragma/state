Right to Left

Why do we reject the right-to-left political model? Most political discourse in the Western world is based on that model. Most people in the Western world seem to be unable to see the world in any other way.

Can people understand our proposed system if we don't explain it in familiar terms? To date, everyone who has read the material has criticized it in terms of that model. When we explain that we aren't interested in right vs. left, people generally can't grasp the concept. It isn't that they disagree; it's more that they literally can't see things any other way.

So, should we find a way to describe our system in those terms? Would that help people at least get started with understanding what we're proposing here? We prefer not to try that, because the whole model is a mis-match. It's better to set it aside and start fresh. If that's difficult for people, then so be it. We've even come to the conclusion that people's belief in the right-to-left model is one of the root causes of the problems in the US and the Western world today.

Fundamental Premise of the State

The proposed State is based on one fundamental premise: Americans want to maximize their individual liberty on a sustainable basis. The right-to-left model is based on political ideology, and does not directly address the practical goal of maximizing individual liberty.

We are not convinced it addresses any practical goal, because of its rigid ideological assumptions. The only goal of an ideologically-driven system is to implement and sustain a particular ideology. The effects on real people in their everyday lives are not important compared to ideological purity.

Our proposed system is entirely based on the effects on real people in their everyday lives, regardless of whether any particular policy or legal statute happens to resemble one that is favored by a particular ideology. So, the right-to-left model does not serve.

Limitations of the Right-to-Left Model

The right-to-left political model is an artifact of European history that has been maintained and evolved within the context of Western civilization. Other humans have developed different worldviews and conceptions of how populations can be governed and how societies can be organized. When people limit their thinking just to this one model, they deny themselves access to ideas that may be useful to them.

A second issue is that when political discussion and debate, and competition in elections, are framed solely in terms of right vs. left ideology, there is a tendency for people to make judgments based on superficial categorization of ideas into a "right" or "left" bucket. This can be problematic when a country or society faces issues that aren't solvable by applying an "ideology."

Examples in the contemporary United States include such diverse challenges as infrastructure restoration and maintenance, homelessness, food and drug safety, healthcare, scientific research, public education, immigration, freedom of religion, and international relations. Practical solutions to these problems may require policies and laws that apply ideas from multiple different ideologies, or from no particular ideology. People who are self-crippled by knee-jerk adherence to an ideology are definitionally incapable of solving such problems.

Exactly what is meant by "left" or "right" has changed with time and place since the wording was first used in the French National Assembly in 1789, when supporters of the monarchy's traditional powers sat on one side of the room and opponents on the other. When you look at it that way, it is clearly not the eternal and infinite definition of All Things Political, but merely a description of a seating arrangement when two opposing factions preferred not to commingle. Does it make sense to base an entire political system on a seating arrangement in a room in France in 1789?

What Questions Do We Ask Instead?

Instead of asking whether any given policy adheres to a particular political ideology, we ask whether it supports or undermines the country's ability to sustain the conditions that enable Americans to exercise their individual liberty. When determining whether a particular action or inaction counts as a "crime," we ask whether it had a harmful effect on anyone.

Often, there is no single, simple answer, but a chain of questions that touch on related issues. As a result, there are limitations on individual liberty. The main one is what we call Kindergarten Wisdom: One person's rights end where the next person's begin. The other one (and yes, there are only two limitations on individual liberty) is that the State's ability to sustain the necessary conditions for liberty must not be undermined. What this boils down to are the Three Pillars of Independence: Self-sufficiency in food, energy, and defense.

The result is a system that has relatively few laws compared with nearly all other systems, and a criminal justice system with few offenses that call for severe punishment. As long as you cause no harm to anyone other than yourself, and you don't undermine the system that enables you to live freely, you can basically do anything you please.

When people ask, "but isn't that a leftist approach?" or "isn't that a right-wing approach?" we shrug, because the question is not only irrelevant, it is a non sequitur - not meaningful enough even to be wrong.
